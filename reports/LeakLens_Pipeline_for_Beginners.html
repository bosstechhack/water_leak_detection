0Before we start

You click a spot on the map, press Analyse, and a few milliseconds later the dashboard says “Probable leak near node 15”. What happened in between?

The most common misunderstandingThe system does not simulate the water network when you press Analyse. The simulation was done earlier, for hundreds of situations, and the results were saved in files. Pressing Analyse makes the computer read the saved results for one situation and think about them. Think of a hospital: the patient charts were recorded earlier, and the doctor reads one chart and gives a diagnosis.

When you click a node on the map (using Pick on map), you are not creating a leak there. You are choosing which saved situation to look at, for example “a leak at node 15”. There is one saved leak situation for every junction in every network.

The whole journey in one line

You choose a situation→Read saved data→Compare with a normal day→Follow the water→14 numbers→Which kind of event?→Where?→Show the answer

We will follow one real example all the way: a leak at node 15 in the network called Net3. It is an unseen test scenario, meaning the system never learned from it. All numbers in green boxes are real numbers from the project.

1Meet the network

A water network is a set of points joined by pipes.

WordPlain meaning
Junction (node)A point where pipes meet and customers take water. Net3 has 92 of them.
PipeCarries water between two points. Water can flow either way.
Reservoir / Tank / PumpA source of water / a storage tank / a machine that pushes water and adds pressure.
PressureHow hard the water pushes inside the pipe. We write it in metres.
FlowHow much water moves through a pipe each second (litres per second).
DemandHow much water the customers at a node use.
Real numbersNet3 has 97 points in total (90 junctions are analysed) and 119 pipes and pumps. Our leak is at node 15. Node 15 is a dead end: it has just one pipe (pipe 151, which joins node 15 to node 143), and almost no customers.

2You choose and press Analyse

Your clicks decide which saved situation is analysed.

  1. You pick the network (NET3) and “what happened” (Leak).
  2. You click Pick on map, then click node 15 (or type 15). The dashboard now filters the list to the leak scenario at node 15. Its name is net3_leak_15.
  3. You press Analyse this scenario.

The browser then sends one small message to the server (the “backend”):

GET /api/analyze/net3_leak_15?use_demand_meters=true

“Please analyse the saved scenario net3_leak_15, and assume customers have smart meters.” The button shows “Analysing…” while it waits.

Why “smart meters”?The whole method depends on knowing how much water customers use at each node. That is what lets us spot water that goes missing. You can untick the option to see how much worse the system does without it.

3The data was recorded earlier

For every saved situation, the simulator wrote three tables, one row for each hour of a 24-hour day.

Pressure table
Pressure (m) at every node, every hour.
Flow table
Flow (litres per second) in every pipe, every hour.
Demand table
Water customers actually received at every node, every hour.

The same three tables also exist for the normal day (the “baseline”: nothing wrong). That is what we compare against.

How was the leak created in the simulation?

A hole of 0.001 m² was opened at node 15 at 12:00. Water escapes through a hole at a speed that depends on the pressure:

Qleak = 0.75 × A × √( 2 × 9.81 × P )A = hole area (m²), P = pressure at the node (m)
Real numbersAt 16:00 the pressure at node 15 is 35.92 m, so the leak loses 0.75 × 0.001 × √(2 × 9.81 × 35.92) = 19.9 litres per second. Keep this number in mind: the system will find it again from the data alone.

Sensor noise: real sensors are never exact. Some scenarios have small random errors added to the saved numbers. The normal-day reference is kept clean.

4Compare with a normal day

First question: did the pressure drop compared with a normal day?

The server reads the files (about 25 rows each) and subtracts, node by node and hour by hour:

residual = normal pressure − scenario pressurea positive residual means the pressure is lower than normal

The blue line is a normal day; the red line is the leak scenario. Both are the same until 12:00, then the red line drops by about 6 to 7 metres. The size of the gap changes through the afternoon, but it stays far above the normal jitter.

Residual (drop) by hour

Is that drop big or small? The “noise unit”

A drop of 0.3 m might just be sensor jitter. To judge, the system first learns how big normal jitter is. It uses 14 normal training days (days with only sensor noise), measures the spread of the pressure differences (σ), and sets a limit:

noise limit τ = max( 0.1 m , 4 × σ )for Net3 this works out to 0.58 m

Then every drop is divided by this limit. The answer is in noise units: 1 means “exactly at the limit”, 10 means “ten times the limit”.

Real numbersThe largest drop at node 15 is 7.88 m. Divided by 0.58 m, that is 13.5 noise units: about eleven times the normal jitter. Far too big to be noise.
Show the “two hours in a row” rule

A drop only counts if it lasts at least two hours in a row, so one weird reading cannot fool the system. Rule: for each hour take the smaller of this hour and the next, then keep the biggest of those.

Toy example: drops 0.1, 0.2, 6.5, 6.7, 6.6, 6.4, 0.3 → smaller-of-pairs: 0.1, 0.2, 6.5, 6.6, 6.4, 0.3 → biggest = 6.6 (counts). A single one-hour spike of 9.0 between 0.2 and 0.3 gives only 0.3 (ignored).

5Follow the water

Second question: is any water disappearing without a customer using it?

Water cannot appear from nowhere or vanish. At every node:

water coming in − water going out = water customers use + water that leaks

If we know what customers use (the demand table), then anything left over must be leaking. We call it unaccounted flow. Play with this toy node:

Unaccounted flow = change in (in − out) − change in customer use

Notice: when customers just use more water, the extra flow is explained by the meters, so unaccounted flow stays 0. Only a leak creates unaccounted flow. This is the key idea of the whole project.

Real numbers: node 15 at 16:00 Node 15 has one pipe (151). On a normal day almost no water moves through it (0 L/s), and customers use almost nothing. In the leak scenario, 19.9 L/s flows into node 15 through that pipe, nothing flows out, and customers still use nothing. So 19.9 L/s vanishes at node 15: unaccounted flow = 0.01991 m³/s = 19.9 L/s. This matches the 19.9 L/s we calculated from the hole, found from the data alone.

The unaccounted flow is also divided by its own noise level, so it too is in noise units. At node 15 it is 10.0 noise units, again about ten times the noise.

Show the maths
divi(t) = Σ over pipes p of sign(p,i) × Qp(t)sign = +1 if the pipe ends at node i (water comes in), −1 if it starts there (water goes out)
ai(t) = [ divi(scenario) − divi(normal) ] − [ Di(scenario) − Di(normal) ]

In the computer this is one matrix multiplication for all nodes at once.

6Do it for every node

Steps 4 and 5 were shown for node 15, but the computer does them for every junction.

That gives two numbers for each of the 90 junctions: its pressure drop and its unaccounted flow, both in noise units. Here are the 10 most interesting nodes for our leak. The dashed red line is the noise limit (1).

Pressure drop (noise units)
Unaccounted flow (noise units)
What to noticePressure dropped at several nodes near the leak, but the unaccounted flow is large at one node only: node 15. That is why unaccounted flow is the best clue to where the leak is, and pressure alone is blurry.

Only 7 of the 90 junctions have a drop above the noise limit, so this event is local, not network-wide.

7Squeeze everything into 14 numbers

Now we have hundreds of node values. To decide the kind of event, we summarise the whole network into 14 numbers.

Each number looks at all the nodes or pipes together and gives one value: the biggest, the share above the limit, or “how concentrated is it in one place?”. They answer four simple questions:

How much pressure was lost, and how widely?
drop_ratio, frac_hit, median_ratio, conc_drop
Did some places gain pressure?
rise_ratio, frac_rise
Did the sources and pipes change?
flow_frac, source_change, tank_change, pump_drop
Is water vanishing? Do the meters explain the flow?
unacc_ratio, unacc_conc, demand_frac, demand_total

8Is anything above the noise?

A quick check: if everything is within the normal jitter, there is nothing to explain.

The system checks: all pressure drops and rises below 1 noise unit? unaccounted flow below 1? almost no pipes with changed flow? no change in customer use? If all are true, the answer is immediately “Normal day” and the analysis stops. That is why normal days do not cause false alarms. (The price: a leak smaller than the noise cannot be seen.)

Real numbersFor our leak, the biggest drop is about 13.5 and the unaccounted flow about 10.0: far above 1. So the check says “something is going on” and the analysis continues.

9Which kind of event is it?

A “random forest” looks at the 14 numbers and votes.

A decision tree is a short list of yes/no questions (for example, “is the unaccounted flow concentrated at one node?”). A random forest is 300 of them, each trained a little differently. They all look at the 14 numbers and each votes for one of seven kinds: normal, leak, demand change, pump/reservoir, tank, blocked pipe, ageing. The share of votes becomes the probability.

VerdictThe top answer gets about 94% of the votes: Leak. That percentage is the “confidence” (High if 85% or more, Medium from 60%).

The forest was trained only on training scenarios. Our node-15 scenario is an unseen test case, so this is an honest answer. Across all unseen test scenarios the forest names the right kind 82.7% of the time. Leaks and demand changes are the easiest to recognise; pump, tank, blockage and ageing are sometimes confused with each other.

10Where is the leak?

Only now, because the verdict is “leak”, the system ranks the nodes.

Each node gets a score from its two values, each first scaled so the biggest in the network equals 1:

score = A + 0.3 × DA = unaccounted flow ÷ the largest unaccounted flow; D = pressure drop ÷ the largest pressure drop

Unaccounted flow is the main clue (it points to one node). Pressure drop only breaks ties, because pressure also drops at neighbouring nodes.

Across all unseen test leaks, the exact node is ranked first 96.7% of the time.

The server packs everything into one reply; the browser turns it into the screen you see.

The analysis itself takes roughly 22 milliseconds (reading the files takes a little more). The first request after the server starts is slower because it loads the network and learns the noise levels once; after that these are remembered.

13Recap and self-check

  1. You choose a saved situation (clicking a node picks which one).
  2. The server reads its saved pressure, flow and demand tables.
  3. Compare with a normal day: pressure drops, in noise units, lasting two hours.
  4. Follow the water: unaccounted flow = vanishing water = leak.
  5. Do this for every node, then summarise into 14 numbers for the whole network.
  6. Quiet check: everything in the noise? then “normal”.
  7. A random forest votes on the kind of event.
  8. If it is a leak, rank nodes by score = A + 0.3 × D.
  9. The server replies; the dashboard draws the verdict, reasons, map and answer key.

Test yourself (click to reveal)

Is the network simulated when you press Analyse?
No. It was simulated earlier. Analyse reads saved results and analyses them.
What is a “noise unit”?
A measurement divided by the learned size of normal sensor jitter. 1 = exactly at the limit; 11 = eleven times the limit.
Why does a demand change not look like a leak?
The extra water is recorded by the customer meters, so the change in flow minus the change in demand (unaccounted flow) stays near zero.
Do the 14 numbers come from one node or the whole network?
The whole network: one set of 14 per scenario. Each number summarises all the nodes or pipes (the biggest value, the share above the limit, and so on).
What decides the “kind of event”, and what decides “where”?
The 14 whole-network numbers, through the random forest, decide the kind. The per-node values (unaccounted flow and pressure drop), through score = A + 0.3 × D, decide where.
What was the forest trained on?
One row of 14 numbers per scenario, for 478 training scenarios (about 78% of all scenarios). The 139 test scenarios were kept aside.
Always rememberAll of this is simulation, not real sensors. The test set is small, and the system relies on demand meters at the nodes.
