1 The project in 2 minutes

LeakLens finds where a water network is leaking, and tells a leak apart from normal changes such as a demand surge, a pump stopping or a tank running low. Everything is tested on computer simulations of three standard networks.

The question we answerWhen pressure and flow change in a water network, is it a leak or a normal change in operation, and if it is a leak, which node is it?

Say it in one breath (elevator pitch)

“We simulate water networks, learn how noisy a normal day looks, then compare every situation with the normal day. By following the water we find any flow that disappears without a customer meter noticing: that is a leak. A random forest names the kind of event, and a simple formula ranks the nodes where the leak probably is. We trained on 80% of the scenarios and tested on the unseen 20%, and show everything on a dashboard.”

2 The core idea: follow the water

A junction behaves like a bank account: money in, money spent by customers, and the rest. Water cannot appear or vanish, so at every junction:

water in − water out = water customers use + water that leaksnet inflow = metered demand + leak flow

If we know what customers use (smart meters), then water that leaves a node with no customer meter behind it must be leaking. We call it unaccounted flow.

EventPressureMetered?Water lost?Unaccounted flow
LeakBiggest drop at one nodeNoYesLarge at one node
Demand changeDrop near heavy useYesNo≈ 0 (meters cancel it)
Pump / reservoir changeWide drop or shift—No≈ 0
Tank changeShift near tank, flow reroutes—No≈ 0
Blocked pipeDown downstream, up upstream—No≈ 0
Pipe ageingSlow, wide loss—No≈ 0
Why this matters in a vivaThis single idea explains why the final localizer is so much better than the first version, and why the system needs demand meters.

Sensor noise

Gaussian errors with fixed seeds are added to the saved readings of “noisy” scenarios: low = 0.05 m on pressure and 0.5% on flows and demands; high = 0.2 m and 2%. The baseline stays clean (it is the model’s expectation). Most non-leak scenarios also have a noisy twin.

Why non-leak events?If we only simulated leaks, the system would call everything a leak. Non-leak events make the problem hard and let us measure false alarms.

7 The pipeline step by step

  1. Simulate. Each scenario is run in WNTR for 24 hours with pressure-dependent demand; pressure, flow and delivered demand are saved hourly. Noisy scenarios get Gaussian noise.
  2. Ignore unreliable readings. A 1% demand probe finds ill-conditioned nodes (swing > 5 m) and hours (90% of nodes swing > 0.5 m). Net3: nodes 61, 601 and hour 21.
  3. Learn the noise from the network’s training normal runs (14 per network): pressure threshold τP = max{0.1 m, 4σP}, plus the noise of each pipe flow, each node’s demand and each node’s unaccounted flow.
  4. Pressure drop with persistence. di = largest drop of node i that lasts two hours in a row, divided by τP. Same for rises gi.
  5. Follow the water. Unaccounted flow ai = change in net inflow − change in metered demand, in noise units.
  6. Summarise into 14 features (Section 6).
  7. Quiet check. If every signal is within noise, the answer is “normal day”.
  8. Cause classification. A random forest outputs probabilities for 7 kinds; the highest is the verdict.
  9. Ranking (only for “leak”). scorei = Ai + 0.3·Di; sort; first = Top-1.
  10. Show it. The dashboard prints the verdict, plain-language reasons built from templates, the ranked nodes and the search region.

8 All the equations in one place

Physics (simulation)

Qleak = Cd · A · √(2 g P)    (Cd = 0.75; A = hole area; P = pressure head)leak flow grows with pressure
Di(t) = Dbase,i · m(t) · fdemand = base × daily pattern × scenario factor
hf = 10.67 · L · Q1.852 / ( C1.852 · d4.87 )Hazen–Williams head loss; ageing lowers C so hf rises

Signals

ri(t) = P⁰i(t) − P¹i(t)residual: normal day − scenario
peak(x) = maxt min{ x(t), x(t+1) }persistence: a change must last two hours in a row
τP = max{ 0.1 m, 4 σP }    di = max{0, peak(ri)} / τP    gi = max{0, peak(−ri)} / τP
divi(t) = Σp sign(p,i) · Qp(t)   (sign = +1 if pipe p ends at i, −1 if it starts there)net inflow of node i from pipe flows
divi = Di + Qleak,i  ⇒  ai(t) = [ Δdivi(t) ] − [ ΔDi(t) ] = Qleak,i(t)unaccounted flow = leak flow
ai (noise units) = max{0, peak(ai(t))} / ( 4 σa,i )

Decision

quiet ⇔ dmax < 1, gmax < 1, amax < 1, flow_frac < 0.1, demand_frac = 0  ⇒  “normal day”
verdict = argmaxk (votes of 300 trees for class k) / 300    confidence: ≥ 0.85 High, ≥ 0.60 Medium, else Low

Ranking

Ai = ai / maxj aj    Di = di / maxj dj    scorei = Ai + 0.3 · DiA is set to 0 for all nodes if max a < 1 (no unaccounted flow above noise)

Metrics

precision = TP/(TP+FP)    recall = TP/(TP+FN)    F1 = 2·P·R/(P+R)    false-alarm rate = FP/(FP+TN)
accuracy = correct / all    macro-F1 = (1/7) Σ F1k    Top-k = (1/N) Σs 1[ rs ≤ k ]    mean hops = (1/N) Σs hops(n̂s, ns)

9 The ML methods used

What is learned and what is notLearned from data: the cause classifier (random forest; decision tree for reading). Calculated from training data, not learned: noise thresholds. Fixed formulas: unaccounted flow, the quiet check and the ranking.

Random forest (the model that gives the verdict)

  • Input: the 14 features of a scenario. Output: a probability for each of 7 kinds (normal, leak, demand change, pump/reservoir, tank, blocked pipe, ageing).
  • How it works: 300 decision trees, each trained on a random sample of the training scenarios and a random subset of features at each split. For a new scenario each tree votes; vote shares are the probabilities.
  • Settings: n_estimators=300, class_weight="balanced" (leaks are about half the data), random_state=0. No tuning.
  • Why a forest: small tabular data (478 training rows), no scaling needed, robust, gives probabilities, harder to over-fit than one tree, easier to defend than a neural network.

Why not deep learning / GNNs?

The dataset is small and tabular, the physics already gives strong features, and interpretability matters for the viva. Graph neural networks and autoencoders appear in our literature review and are future work; we did not test them.

Rules of the split

  • Learned from training only: noise thresholds (from 14 training normal runs per network) and the forest/tree.
  • Measured on test only: every accuracy, precision, recall, F1, false-alarm, Top-k and hop figure.
  • Grouped: all leaks at one junction (every size, start time, noise level, hidden-behind variant) go to the same side, and a scenario goes with its noisy twin. This prevents data leakage: a test case that is an almost-copy of a training case.
  • Stratified: each network and each kind of event is split about 80/20 (so the test set has 12 of every non-leak kind).
  • The dashboard tags every scenario Unseen test or Training.

Extra checks

  • Train vs test accuracy: 95.6% vs 82.7% → partial memorisation by the forest.
  • Leave-one-network-out: train on two networks, test on the third network’s test scenarios (Section 12).
  • Training-leak Top-1 of the ranking: 97.8% on 231 leaks (almost equal to 96.7% on test, as it is a formula).
Honest caveatsSingle split; small test set; the ranking weight 0.3 and the quiet-check cut-offs were chosen while looking at all scenarios, before the split, and were not re-tuned; earlier we used 3-fold cross-validation (89.8%), replaced because near-copies leaked between folds.

11 How the ranking is done

  1. Only runs if the verdict is leak.
  2. For every analysed junction compute ai (unaccounted flow, noise units) and di (pressure drop, noise units).
  3. Scale each to 0–1: Ai = ai/max a, Di = di/max d. (If max a < 1, A is 0 for all.)
  4. scorei = Ai + 0.3·Di.
  5. Sort descending. First = Top-1. The next four are the “where to look” list.
  6. Search region = top node + its directly connected neighbours.
  7. Error (for scoring): hops = pipes on the shortest path to the true node; metres = pipe length on that path.
Why flow leads
Unaccounted flow is zero everywhere except the leak, so it points to one node. Pressure drops spread to neighbours, so pressure alone is blurry (Top-1 60.0%).
Why pressure at all
It breaks ties when flow signals are weak or noisy. Weight 0.3 is a design choice, not optimised.
Neighbour smoothing
Tested (+0.3 × neighbours’ average). No gain (96.7%), so removed.
The mistake that taught us scalingOur first version added pressure and flow in raw noise units. In Net2, a leak at node 2 had unaccounted flow 11.4 at node 2, but node 1 showed a pressure drop of 128, so node 1 won. Scaling each signal to 0–1 fixed it (Top-1 84.9% → 95.9%).

What the results mean

  • Unaccounted flow is what makes localization work: Top-1 58.3% → 96.7%.
  • Without demand meters Top-1 is 93.3%; pressure alone 60.0%.
  • Cause accuracy (82.7%) is lower than training accuracy (95.6%): the forest partly memorises.
  • Leaks and demand changes are recognised reliably; pump, tank, blockage and ageing overlap.

17 Limitations (say them before they are asked)

  • Simulation only; real networks have model errors, real noise and events we did not simulate.
  • Small test set (139 scenarios, 60 scored leaks, 12 normal days) and a single split.
  • Ranking weight and quiet-check cut-offs were chosen before the split and not re-tuned.
  • Per-node demand meters are assumed; without them accuracy drops.
  • Leaks inside the sensor noise are called normal; the biggest leaks are hardest to pinpoint.
  • Pump, tank, blockage and ageing are confused and do not transfer well to unseen networks.
  • Net3 nodes 61 and 601 and hour 21 are ignored. Pump speed changes not simulated. Only single leaks.

Basics

What is this project in one sentence?
LeakLens is a simulation-based system that looks at pressure, pipe-flow and customer-demand data from a water network, tells whether it is a leak or a normal change (demand, pump, tank, blocked pipe, ageing), and for a leak ranks the nodes where it most probably is. A web dashboard shows the answer in plain language.
What problem are you solving, and why is it hard?
Leaks waste treated water, but a pressure drop alone does not prove a leak: a hydrant, a pump stopping or a tank running low also lowers pressure. So we must (1) detect abnormal behaviour, (2) decide what kind of event it is, and (3) find where a leak is. Sensors are noisy and most utilities cannot put a sensor on every pipe.
What is the title and the short name?
The project title is “Water Leak Localization System”. The short name used in the dashboard and reports is LeakLens.
Why simulation and not real data?
Real leaks do not come with ground truth, and utilities rarely share data. In simulation we create thousands of labelled situations (we know exactly what we changed and where), so we can measure accuracy objectively. The cost is that results are not proof of field performance; we say so explicitly.
What is your contribution if EPANET and the techniques already exist?
Not a new algorithm. The contribution is the integration and a controlled, reproducible study: noise-aware detection, a flow-balance localizer, cause classification (leak versus other events) on three networks with an honest hold-out test, and an explainable dashboard. This matches the positioning in our literature review.

Hydraulics

What are EPANET and WNTR?
EPANET is the standard hydraulic simulator for pressurised pipe networks (it solves flow and pressure for given demands, pumps, tanks and reservoirs). WNTR (Water Network Tool for Resilience) is a Python package from the US EPA that loads EPANET input files and lets us add leaks, controls, pump outages and run extended-period simulations from code.
What is pressure-dependent demand (PDD)?
In ordinary demand-driven analysis customers always receive their full demand. With PDD the delivered demand falls when pressure is low. We use PDD so that behaviour is realistic when a leak or pump outage lowers pressure.
How is a leak modelled?
As an orifice: Q = Cd · A · √(2gP) with discharge coefficient Cd = 0.75, hole area A, gravity g and pressure head P at the node. The leak flow depends on pressure, so low-lying, high-pressure nodes lose more water.
What are the three networks and why those?
Net1 (9 junctions, 1 pump, 1 tank, 1 reservoir), Net2 (35 junctions, 1 tank, no pump or reservoir) and Net3 (92 junctions, 2 pumps, 3 tanks, 2 reservoirs). They are standard EPANET benchmark networks of different sizes and structure, so we can test whether the method generalises.
Why are Net3 nodes 61 and 601 and hour 21 ignored?
A tiny 1% demand change makes these nodes swing by more than 5 m (pump-switching artefacts: a control switches a pump on a different hour). Their pressure is not a reliable signal, so they are excluded from analysis. Leaks at those nodes are therefore not scored in localization. We say so in the limitations.
Why was pump speed not simulated?
WNTR’s own simulator only supports pump speed 1.0. We simulated pump outages (switching a pump off) instead.

Scenarios

What scenarios does the project cover?
617 scenarios on three networks: leaks (299), demand changes (60), pump or reservoir changes (54), tank changes (60), blocked or throttled pipes (48), pipe ageing (42) and normal days (54). Leaks cover every junction, five sizes, four start times, sensor noise, and leaks hidden behind another event.
Why do you include non-leak events?
Because the real question is “leak or not?”. If we only simulated leaks the system would call everything a leak. Non-leak events are exactly what makes the problem hard, and they let us measure false alarms.
What are “noisy” scenarios and how is noise added?
We add Gaussian random errors with fixed seeds to the saved readings: low noise = 0.05 m on pressure and 0.5% on flows and demands; high noise = 0.2 m and 2%. The baseline (normal-day reference) stays clean because it represents the hydraulic model’s expectation.
What does “hidden behind another event” mean?
A leak that happens together with a demand surge, a hydrant, a closed pipe or a pump outage. It tests whether the system still finds the leak when other things also change. Because those events are metered or do not lose water, the unaccounted-flow signal still points to the leak.
Why five leak sizes?
To measure sensitivity: from tiny (0.0001 m²) to burst (0.01 m²). Tiny leaks sit near the sensor noise; very large ones lower pressure everywhere, which makes the exact node hard to pick.

Data

What is in the dataset? What are the features?
For each scenario we save hourly pressure at every node, flow in every pipe and delivered demand at every node (25 hourly rows). From these we compute 14 features per scenario (drop_ratio, frac_hit, median_ratio, conc_drop, rise_ratio, frac_rise, flow_frac, source_change, tank_change, pump_drop, unacc_ratio, unacc_conc, demand_frac, demand_total). The classifier’s input is a table of 617 rows × 14 features, with the true event kind as the label.
Which features matter most?
By random-forest importance the top ones are unacc_conc 12.0%, unacc_ratio 9.3%, demand_frac 9.1%, rise_ratio 8.9%. Unaccounted-flow features and demand evidence dominate because they separate leaks from demand changes. Importance values are spread fairly evenly, so no single feature carries the model.
Is the label ever given to the model as input?
No. The true event kind and leak node are stored separately and only used for training labels (training scenarios) and for scoring (test scenarios). The 14 features are computed purely from pressure, flow and demand.
Why are features divided by noise thresholds?
So that “1” always means “at the noise limit”. That makes features comparable across networks with different sizes and different noise levels, and prevents tiny absolute numbers from being treated as large.

Method

Explain the full pipeline step by step.
1) Simulate scenarios and save data. 2) Learn the noise level of each network from normal training runs. 3) Compare each scenario with the normal day: pressure drops (kept only if they last 2 hours) and unaccounted flow. 4) Summarise into 14 features. 5) Quiet check: if everything is within noise, the answer is normal. 6) Otherwise a random forest names the kind of event. 7) If it is a leak, rank the junctions by score = A + 0.3·D. 8) The dashboard shows the verdict, reasons and the ranked nodes.
What is “unaccounted flow” and why does it equal the leak flow?
At a junction water must balance: net inflow = customer demand + leak. Subtract the change in metered demand from the change in net inflow and what remains is the leak flow. Formula: a_i(t) = [Δdiv_i(t)] − [ΔD_i(t)] = Q_leak,i(t). It is zero everywhere except at the leak node (in noise-free data). A demand change cancels because it is metered.
Does the method need demand meters? What if they do not exist?
Yes, per-node demand data is assumed. Without them, a demand change at another node looks like a small leak: localization Top-1 falls to 93.3% and cause accuracy to 74.1%, and on an unseen Net3 cause accuracy falls to 14.5%. This is a stated limitation and a Semester 2 item (estimate demands, use district meters).
Why persistence of two hours?
To ignore one-off noisy readings. A residual counts only if it stays above threshold for two consecutive hours: peak = max over t of min{x(t), x(t+1)}.
How is the noise threshold learned and why 4σ?
From the network’s training normal runs (14 per network): τ_P = max{0.1 m, 4·σ_P}, σ_P being the standard deviation of pressure residuals. For Gaussian noise a 4σ limit has a very small chance of being crossed by chance (about 3 in 100,000 per reading), and the 2-hour persistence rule makes false alarms rarer still. It gave τ_P ≈ 0.50, 0.53, 0.58 m for Net1, Net2, Net3. The factor 4 was a design choice and was not tuned.
What is the “quiet check”?
If drop_ratio < 1, rise_ratio < 1, unacc_ratio < 1, flow_frac < 0.1 and demand_frac = 0, every signal is within noise, so the answer is “normal day” without calling the classifier. It prevents false alarms on normal days. A side effect: leaks smaller than the noise cannot be detected.

ML

Which machine-learning models did you use and why?
A random forest (300 decision trees, balanced class weights, seed 0) for the verdict, plus a depth-4 decision tree for readable rules. Reasons: small tabular data (478 training rows, 14 numeric features), no need for feature scaling, handles mixed patterns, gives class probabilities, and trees are easier to explain than neural networks. Deep learning would overfit such a small dataset and be harder to defend.
What is a random forest?
Many decision trees, each trained on a random sample of the training scenarios and a random subset of features at each split. For a new scenario every tree votes; the share of votes for each class is its probability, and the highest wins. Averaging many different trees reduces the over-fitting of a single tree.
What is a decision tree?
A chain of yes/no questions on the features, ending in a class. Ours (depth 4) is saved in decision_rules.txt, for example “if unacc_conc > 0.37 then leak”. It is easy to read but less accurate (69.1% test accuracy versus 82.7% for the forest).
What does “balanced class weights” do?
Leaks are about half the scenarios. Balanced weights make errors on rare kinds cost more during training, so the forest does not simply favour “leak”.
Is the localization (ranking) machine learning?
No. The ranking is a fixed formula, score = A + 0.3·D, with no training. Only the cause classifier (verdict) is learned from data; the noise thresholds are calculated from the training normal runs.
Did you tune hyper-parameters?
No. Number of trees, depth, the 0.3 weight, the 4σ factor and the quiet-check cut-offs were fixed during development. The 0.3 weight and the cut-offs were chosen while looking at all scenarios, before the split existed, and were not re-tuned afterwards. We state this as a limitation.
What other models did you consider?
We compared a decision tree and a random forest. We did not test SVMs, gradient boosting, k-NN, neural networks, autoencoders with a one-class SVM, or graph neural networks. That is future work, and a quick comparison on the same split is easy to add.
What does the “confidence” on the dashboard mean?
It is the forest’s top class probability (share of trees voting for it): High ≥ 85%, Medium ≥ 60%, otherwise Low. It is not a calibrated probability and not a guarantee.

Training & testing

How did you train and test? What is the split?
All 617 scenarios were split once (seed 42) into 478 for training (about 77.5%) and 139 for testing (22.5%). Noise thresholds and the classifier use training scenarios only; every reported result is measured on the test scenarios, which the system never saw while learning.
Why is the split not exactly 80/20?
Because near-copies are kept together (grouped split). All leaks at one junction (every size, start time, noise and hidden-behind variant) go to the same side, and a scenario goes with its noisy twin. Group sizes vary, so the share is 77.5% / 22.5% rather than exactly 80/20.
Why group the split? What is data leakage?
If the same leak node at a slightly different size is in both training and test, the test scenario is almost a copy of a training one and the score is inflated. That is data leakage. Grouping prevents it.
Why not test on the training data?
A model can score perfectly by memorising. Our evidence: cause accuracy is 95.6% on training scenarios but 82.7% on unseen ones. The test shows true generalisation.
Did you use cross-validation?
Earlier we used 3-fold cross-validation on all scenarios (cause accuracy 89.8%), but near-duplicates leaked between folds, so it was optimistic. We replaced it with the hold-out split (82.7%). We also run leave-one-network-out (train on two networks, test on the third).
What is leave-one-network-out and what does it show?
Train on the training scenarios of two networks and test on the third network’s test scenarios. Accuracy: Net1 48.5%, Net2 81.8%, Net3 54.8%. Leaks carry over to a new network (leak recall up to 100%), but telling pump, tank, blockage and ageing apart does not carry over well.
Is the ranking trained or tested on the same data?
The ranking is a formula, so there is nothing to train. Its Top-1 is 97.8% on the 231 training leaks and 96.7% on the 60 test leaks, nearly identical, as expected. But the weight 0.3 was chosen while looking at all scenarios.

Metrics

Explain precision, recall and F1.
Precision = TP/(TP+FP): when the system raises an alarm, how often it is real. Recall = TP/(TP+FN): of real problems, how many it catches. F1 = 2PR/(P+R): high only if both are high. For our full detector: TP=119, FP=0, FN=8, TN=12 → precision 100.0%, recall 93.7%, F1 96.7%.
What is the false-alarm rate and why 0%?
FP/(FP+TN): the share of normal days wrongly flagged. Ours is 0.0% (0 of 12 normal test days) versus 41.7% for a fixed 0.5 m alarm. With only 12 normal test days the true rate is not exactly zero; the result shows it is low, not that it is impossible.
How is accuracy calculated, and what is macro-F1?
Accuracy = correct / total = 115/139 = 82.7%. Macro-F1 is the plain average of the seven per-kind F1 scores (76.4%), so weak small kinds pull it down; accuracy can hide them.
How is Top-1 calculated?
For each test leak we sort junctions by score and find the position r of the true node. Top-1 = share of leaks with r = 1; Top-k = share with r ≤ k. Ours: 58/60 = 96.7% (first version 35/60 = 58.3%). One miss moves it by about 1.7 points.
What are “hops” and metres error?
Hops = number of pipes on the shortest path between the system’s first guess and the true node (0 = exact). Metres = total pipe length on that path. Mean error for the final method is 0.13 hops (about 60 m); 96.7% of leaks are found at the node or its direct neighbour.
What is a confusion matrix?
A table whose rows are the true kind and columns what the system said. The diagonal is correct answers. Ours (139 test scenarios) shows leaks and demand changes are recognised reliably; pump, tank, blockage and ageing are confused with each other.
What is “end-to-end” success?
A leak counts only if the verdict is “leak” and the true node is ranked first: 96.7%.

Ranking

How is the ranking done?
For each junction, A = its unaccounted flow ÷ the largest unaccounted flow in the network, D = its pressure drop ÷ the largest drop; score = A + 0.3·D. Nodes are sorted by score; the first is Top-1, the next ones are where to look if it is wrong. If no node has unaccounted flow above noise, only the pressure term is used.
Why does unaccounted flow lead and pressure only break ties?
Unaccounted flow is exactly zero except at the leak, so it points to one node. Pressure drops spread to neighbours (a node next to the leak also drops), so pressure alone is blurry: Top-1 60.0% versus 96.7%. An early version added both in raw units, and pressure drowned the flow signal (84.9% Top-1); scaling each to 0–1 fixed it.
Why 0.3?
A design choice: enough weight on pressure to break ties when flow signals are weak, small enough that unaccounted flow dominates. It was chosen during development, not re-tuned after the split, and not optimised.
Why did neighbour smoothing not help?
Smoothing adds a fraction of the neighbours’ scores, assuming pressure drops spread along pipes. But unaccounted flow already pins one node, so smoothing blurs a sharp signal. On the test leaks it gave 96.7% (same as without). We removed it; network distance is used instead for the error metric and the search region.
What is the “search region”?
The predicted node plus the nodes directly connected to it. If the leak is not at the first guess it is most likely here, so a crew can check these next.
Where is network (graph) distance used?
Hops and metres are computed with networkx shortest paths on the pipe graph, to measure localization error and to define the search region. We did not use it inside the score because smoothing gave no benefit.

Results

Summarise your main results.
On unseen test scenarios: exact leak node first 96.7% (58/60) versus 58.3% for the first version; real leak in the first five 98.3%; false alarms 0.0% versus 41.7%; right cause 82.7% (95.6% on training); about 22 ms per scenario.
Why is cause accuracy lower than leak accuracy?
Leaks have a unique fingerprint (unaccounted flow). Pump, tank, blockage and ageing all lower pressure over a wide area without losing water, so their fingerprints overlap, especially in the small Net1. Some small events are also reported as normal because they stay within noise.
Which cases fail?
(1) Leaks inside the sensor noise (called normal). (2) The largest leaks (0.01 m²): pressure collapses everywhere and the exact node is hard to pick (Top-1 50% on four test cases). (3) Pump, tank, blockage and ageing mistaken for each other. (4) Leaks at the ignored Net3 nodes 61/601 are not found (7 test leaks; leak recall would be 100% without them).
Is 96.7% Top-1 trustworthy?
It is measured on unseen scenarios, but with only 60 test leaks one miss is 1.7 points, the split was made once, and the weight was chosen before the split. So treat it as “very high in simulation”, not as an exact figure.
Which result shows the system is better than the earlier version?
Same test leaks: Top-1 58.3% → 96.7%, Top-5 71.7% → 98.3%, average miss 3.35 → 0.13 pipes, false alarms 41.7% → 0%.

Software

What does the backend do and why FastAPI/SQLite?
FastAPI exposes endpoints (/api/networks, /scenarios, /network, /analyze/{id}, /evaluation), validates responses with Pydantic and runs the ML pipeline. SQLite (via SQLAlchemy) stores the scenario catalogue (id, network, group, kind, truth nodes, parameters, train/test tag): small metadata, no server needed. Our slides said PostgreSQL; the implementation uses SQLite and we corrected the slides.
What does the frontend do and which technologies?
A Next.js 16 / React 19 TypeScript dashboard: Tailwind CSS 4 with colour tokens for light/dark themes, React Flow for the network map, Recharts for charts, axios for API calls and lucide icons. It has a guided scenario picker, a plain-language verdict, an Unseen test / Training tag, and Performance and Guide tabs.
Are the “How do we know?” sentences written by an AI?
No. They are fixed templates that appear when a feature passes a limit (e.g. “a pump lost 100% of its flow” when pump_drop > 0.5), with real values filled in.
How long does an analysis take?
About 22 ms to analyse one scenario (signals, features and ranking) once its data are loaded, so it is suitable for interactive use.

Limits

What are the limitations?
Simulation only; small test set and single split; ranking weight and cut-offs chosen before the split; per-node demand meters assumed; leaks inside noise are called normal; largest leaks hard to pinpoint; pump/tank/blockage/ageing confused and not transferable across networks; Net3 nodes 61/601 and hour 21 ignored; pump speed changes not simulated; single leak only.
Would it work on a real network?
Unknown. A real deployment needs a calibrated hydraulic model for the baseline, sensor noise estimates and demand data, and real networks have model errors and events we did not simulate. Our results are simulation-based by design.
What about multiple simultaneous leaks, missing or sparse sensors?
Not handled in Semester 1 (our slides place them in Semester 2). Unaccounted flow would show several nodes, but we did not evaluate that. Sparse or missing sensors need methods like centrality-based placement (Leonzio et al.).
What would you do with more time?
Compare more models on the same split; repeat the split many times for error bars; estimate demands without nodal meters; handle multiple leaks and missing sensors; make cause classification transfer across networks (graph or algorithm-informed models); validate on public benchmarks such as LeakDB or BattLeDIM.

Tricky

Your cause accuracy is 82.7% but training accuracy is 95.6%. Is that a problem?
It shows partial memorisation (over-fitting) of the cause model, which is why we report test accuracy. It is expected with 478 rows. The leak ranking has no such gap because it is not trained.
Could a leak and a demand change be confused?
Yes if demand meters are missing. With metered demand the extra flow of a demand change cancels, so unaccounted flow stays near zero; the classifier therefore separates them (demand change F1 100% on the test set). Without meters demand changes look like small leaks.
Why did leak recall drop to 89.6%?
All seven test leaks that were not named as leaks are at Net3 node 601, an ignored pump-outlet node. Every other test leak was named a leak.
Why did you not use deep learning or GNNs?
The dataset is small (478 training rows), tabular and physics-informed; deep models would overfit and be hard to explain. Our literature review treats GNNs as an option after a strong interpretable baseline exists.
Is “normal” detection really zero false alarms or an artefact of the gate?
Both contribute: the learned threshold plus the quiet check. Normal scenarios have only sensor noise, so all signals stay under 1 noise unit and the gate returns normal. The false-alarm rate is measured on 12 held-out normal days, and noise thresholds came from different (training) normal runs.
How do you know the split did not favour you?
We cannot fully exclude it with one split: it is a fixed seed, grouped and stratified, and not tuned. The honest improvement would be repeated random splits with confidence intervals, which we list as next work.
